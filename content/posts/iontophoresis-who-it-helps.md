Wednesday night. The trays are already filled. You sit down, put your hands in the water, set a phone timer, and feel that familiar tingle climb into a mild sting. Somewhere in the house someone asks if you are free in twenty minutes. You are not. That block of the night is already taken.

## What a tray night actually costs

This is maintenance, not a one-off experiment. You have already done the early weeks of sitting here several times a week. Tonight is the quieter version: keep the dryness from slipping, or watch wet palms come back after a stretch of skipped sessions<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>.

The setup is simple and unglamorous. Hands or feet sit in shallow trays of tap water with electrodes. A mild current runs through the water. Many protocols reverse polarity mid-session so both sides get exposure<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>. The current is believed to temporarily plug sweat ducts and change the local electrochemical picture enough that less sweat reaches the surface for a while<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>. You do not need a lecture to feel what that means in your skin: warmth, pins-and-needles, sometimes a sting you can live with and sometimes one you cannot.

Induction often looks like several sessions a week for about twenty to thirty minutes at a time, with dryness showing up after a stretch of those visits rather than after night one<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>. Maintenance is more irregular: sometimes weekly, sometimes every few weeks, always temporary. Miss too long and you are back near the beginning<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>.

That is the calendar tax. The phone timer is not optional decoration. It is the whole treatment sitting in your kitchen while dinner waits.

Some nights the sting is easy to ignore. Some nights you watch the clock and bargain with yourself about cutting the session short. Either way, that block of the night is gone. That is the part brochure language skips.

## Who the grind actually fits

Iontophoresis earns its place mainly for focal palms and soles, because the limbs can go in the trays. Underarms are a secondary adaptation at best. Clinically it often shows up after topical aluminum chloride fails or cannot be tolerated, as a non-invasive option for moderate to severe hand or foot hyperhidrosis<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>.

The people it fits are usually willing to trade evenings for predictability. They can do a multi-week induction without needing an overnight miracle. They can tolerate mild local skin cost: tingling, redness, dryness, the occasional worse sting<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>. They treat the device like a recurring appointment with themselves, not like a product that should erase the problem forever.

There are also class-level reasons not to force it. Pregnancy, implantable electronics such as a pacemaker or defibrillator, epilepsy, and metal implants in the current path are the usual soft stops clinicians flag<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>. Those are not vibe checks. They are safety boundaries.

If your hyperhidrosis is mostly face or trunk, or you need a treatment that does not sit on a weekly calendar, this grind is already pointing the wrong direction.

Kids can be candidates in specialized settings, and comfort matters even more there<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>. The same rule still applies: if the session itself becomes the enemy, the dryness will not save the plan.

## Who it drains (and why quitting isn’t failure)

Time-poor people get drained first. Forty minutes with wet hands in trays is not “just a quick thing” when work, kids, travel, or exhaustion already own the night. Treatment fatigue is a real long-term bottleneck with methods that depend on repeated in-person sessions<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>.

The sensation drains another group. Mild sting is common. If every session feels like an ordeal, adherence dies even when the dryness was real.

Wrong site drains people too. Trays are a limb tool. Stretching them into a universal answer creates shame when the map does not match.

And it drains people who get a good run, then life interrupts, then wetness returns, then they decide they “failed iontophoresis.” That sequence is how a temporary effect behaves when maintenance stops<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>. Quitting because the calendar won is information about fit. It is not proof you lacked discipline.

From lived coaching judgment, the honest split is simple. Keep the trays if palms or soles are the site, you can tolerate the sessions, and you are building a long game after topicals. Stop without shame if the evenings cost more than the dryness returns, if the sting is too much, or if you needed a different site treated. Fewer sweaty days is a quality-of-life aim. It is not a demand that every tool work for every person forever.

The trays either fit your life or they drain it. Naming that early is the useful part. You can respect a tool and still decide it belongs to someone else's week, not yours.
