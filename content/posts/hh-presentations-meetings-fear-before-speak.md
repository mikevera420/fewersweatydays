You are standing at the front of a conference room, or you have just unmuted on a video call with your camera on, and you are holding a few printed pages of notes. By the second slide the corner of the top page, where your thumb is, has gone damp and started to curl. You move the notes to your other hand and wipe the first hand on your pants leg below the edge of the table, where you hope nobody can see. When you look down to find your place, you have lost the line you were on.

For a lot of people with hyperhidrosis, that minute is the hard part of a presentation. They often know the material well, and what they remember afterward is how much of their attention went to their hands. This piece goes through the things in the room one at a time: what is in your hand, the shirt you are wearing, the handshake at the end, and what you can say if someone in the meeting notices.

## The notes, clicker, or pen in your hand

Paper is usually the first thing to show it. Printed notes held in a damp hand get soft where your fingers are, and the corner you keep gripping starts to wrinkle and curl. If you wrote anything by hand, the ink can smear under your thumb, so the reminder you wrote at the top of the page is harder to read by the time you get to it. Mike Vera, who runs this site, remembers this from school: "Imagine my embarrassment in school with my sweaty hands soaking through my notes and tests."

The clicker for the slides can get slick in the same way. You press the button and wonder if it registered, or you grip it harder so it does not slip, and then your hand sweats more around it. If you are presenting from a laptop, the trackpad can stop reading a wet finger well, and the cursor jumps or does not move at all. A whiteboard marker gets slippery too.

All of this happens while you are also trying to speak to a room. Part of your mind is on the next point, and part of it is checking whether the paper looks wet, whether anyone saw you wipe your hand, and whether the clicker will work on the next press. That is tiring, and it is a big reason people with sweaty hands dread presenting.

There is a reason it gets worse as the meeting goes on. Being nervous sets off more sweating, and noticing the sweating makes people more nervous, so the two keep feeding each other.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> In one small study, people with hyperhidrosis sweated more than other people during a mock job interview in front of a panel of judges, even though they did not rate themselves as more stressed. Mike puts the same thing in his own words when he talks about being "nervous for a job interview or a presentation," and then adding on top of that the fact that "your hands are leaking like a water fountain."

What people do about the paper and the clicker is mostly simple and practical. A lot of people hold their notes by the very edge or a top corner so less of the page touches their palm, or they set the notes down on the podium and only pick them up to turn a page. A tissue or a folded paper towel in a pocket helps too, for drying your hand once before you stand up and again when there is a natural pause. On video calls, people often keep their notes on the screen or taped next to the camera so there is no paper in their hands to begin with.

## The shirt under the lights

The second thing people think about is the shirt. Conference rooms are often warm, with bright overhead lights and the projector running. When you raise your arm to point at something on the screen, the underarm of your shirt is in plain view. If you have been sweating through the first few slides, there may be a dark mark there, and sometimes a line down the middle of your back that people sitting behind you can see when you turn to the screen.

Because of this, a lot of people plan the shirt the night before a presentation. They pick a color and fabric that they already know shows less. Some keep a jacket or a cardigan on for the whole meeting, even when the room is warm and everyone else has taken theirs off. The jacket can make you warmer, but a lot of people would rather be warm than have the shirt show.

Some people also change how they point. They use the clicker's laser or the mouse cursor so they do not have to lift an arm toward the screen, or they gesture with their hand lower and closer to their body.

Part of what makes sweat feel so bad in a meeting is that other people can see it. One review of research on anxiety (it looked at anxiety disorders in general and was not a study of hyperhidrosis) noted that because sweat is visible to others, it tends to feed the worry about it.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>

## The handshake when it ends

When the meeting wraps up, people often walk over to say thanks or ask a question, and some of them put out a hand. By then your hand has been holding paper and a clicker for half an hour and it is already wet. People deal with this in a few plain ways. They wipe their palm on their pant leg as they walk over, they keep a tissue in the hand they are not using, or they say they need to grab some water and wash their hands in the restroom before talking.

Mike has talked about the handshake from the other end, before a job interview rather than after a meeting. He describes sitting in the lobby, a little nervous, with hands that are "a little sweaty and that's only making you more nervous," and how knowing "you're going to have to shake someone's hand is making you more anxious." He also says, "I'm pretty sure I've lost some job opportunities because of my awful handshake."

If the handshake is the part you dread most, there is a separate post on [wet palms and handshakes](/blog/palmar-hh-handshake) that goes into it in more detail.

## What you can say if someone notices in the meeting

Most of the time nobody says anything. Once in a while someone does notice, maybe the person next to you sees the damp corner of your notes or a mark on your shirt when you sit back down. When that happens in the middle of a meeting, you do not need to explain anything, and the easiest thing is to get back to the meeting.

People usually handle it in small ways. They say "it's warm in here" and keep going, or make a short joke and move to the next slide. Plenty of people say nothing at all and carry on with what they were saying, and the person who noticed usually moves on quickly too.

Mike's general approach to embarrassing moments points the same way. He has said that instead of running off to hide, he would rather "shrug it off and actually make a joke about it." He was talking about his attitude in general, and it carries over to a moment in a meeting that lasts a few seconds.

It also helps to remember what everyone else in that room is doing. Most of them are thinking about their own part of the meeting, the slides they still have to present, the question they want to ask, or the email they need to send after. They are paying much less attention to your hands and your shirt than you are.

So if you want something ready for your next meeting, keep it short and plain. "It's warm in here, let me keep going" is enough, and then go back to your next point while everyone else goes back to thinking about their own slides.
