If you are weighing endoscopic thoracic sympathectomy (ETS), or living with what came after, you have probably heard two stories at once: dry hands that finally work in public, and a new kind of sweat somewhere else that nobody spent enough time explaining.

Both can be true. This piece is about that tradeoff in plain language — not a scare campaign, and not a sales pitch for surgery.

## What ETS is (in plain language)

ETS is a surgery that interrupts part of the sympathetic nerve chain in the chest so the original high-sweat sites (often palms, underarms, or face) get less of the “turn on” signal<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>. Surgeons may cut, clip, or cauterize at chosen levels depending on where the hyperhidrosis is worst<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>.

It sits near the top of the treatment ladder for severe focal hyperhidrosis after topicals, devices, injections, and medicines have failed or do not fit<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>. The intended win is dryness where life was hardest. The body does not always stop there.

Exact level maps belong in a surgical consult, not a blog checklist. What matters for readers is the idea: you are changing an autonomic pathway on purpose, not flipping a simple “off” switch for sweat everywhere.

## What “compensatory sweating” actually means

Compensatory sweating (also called compensatory hyperhidrosis) means new excess sweat in areas the surgery did not target — often trunk, back, abdomen, or thighs — after the original sites quiet down<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup>.

Day to day, that can look like a dry handshake paired with a soaked shirt, damp lower back in meetings, or thighs that force wardrobe workarounds you never had before. In one long-term series, people with compensatory sweating commonly mapped it to the back, thighs, and abdomen<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-3">[3]</a></sup>.

A related but different operation is lumbar endoscopic sympathectomy (ELS) for plantar sweating. Compensatory patterns after lumbar work can also shift in ways people did not expect<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-4">[4]</a></sup>. That is not the same procedure as thoracic ETS — keep the labels straight when you read forums or papers.

Compensatory sweating is not a rare footnote. It is the main tradeoff people mean when they say “ETS worked, but…”

## How common it is — and why one global % lies

Rates vary hard by how surgeons define compensatory sweating, which levels they treat, clipping versus cutting, adult versus pediatric cohorts, and how long they follow people. Consensus and older surgical literature already describe a wide published range rather than one tidy worldwide number<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-5">[5]</a></sup>.

Named series help more than fake globals:

- In one adult follow-up cohort, about **97.6%** of respondents reported compensatory sweating, while overall satisfaction stayed high (around **94%**). Even among people with more severe compensatory grades, many still said they were satisfied<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-3">[3]</a></sup>.
- In one comparative series, treating more thoracic levels (T2–T4 versus T2–T3) came with a higher compensatory sweating rate **in that series** (**96%** vs **44%**)<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-6">[6]</a></sup>. That is a signal about extent, not a universal law for every operating room.
- In a clipping series (ETSC), compensatory sweating was reported around **50.8%**, with high would-repeat satisfaction<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-7">[7]</a></sup>. Clipping is not identical to cutting — label it when you cite it.
- In a pediatric series, about **95%** reached complete palmar dryness, about **66%** reported compensatory sweating, and **99%** said they would repeat the choice<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-8">[8]</a></sup>. Kids are not adults; keep that label on.

One review cites severe compensatory sweating after sympathectomy in the ballpark of about **35%** in its framing, versus much lower rates after more limited approaches<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-9">[9]</a></sup>. That is a severe-CH ballpark in that review — not a claim that “35% of people worldwide regret ETS.”

So if someone sells you a single percentage for “everyone,” ask which series, which definition, and which severity they mean.

## Living with the tradeoff (without hype or fear)

Compensatory sweating has grades. Clinical writing describes a spectrum from mild and tolerable through severe patterns that can include deep regret language at the highest grade, plus practical coping like breathable clothes and towels when trunk sweat takes over<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-10">[10]</a></sup>.

The honest pair is this: many people get the dryness they came for, and many also get new sweat geography. High satisfaction in several series can sit next to high compensatory rates without either fact canceling the other<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-3">[3]</a></sup><sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-8">[8]</a></sup>. That is an autonomic tradeoff, not a morality play.

If you are deciding, treat ETS as irreversible pathway change with a common redistribution risk. If you already had it, you are not weak for noticing the new map. You are describing a known outcome category.

From lived coaching judgment, the useful frame is no cure myth and no fear theater: understand what the surgery changes, what it does not guarantee, and what non-surgical tools still exist for people who never want that tradeoff — including clinical ladders that stop short of sympathectomy, and iontophoresis as a **class** of lower-stakes maintenance for palms and soles when it fits. Lower-stakes is not “no side effects.”

ETS can be the right choice for some severe cases. Compensatory sweating is still part of the real conversation — in plain language, before anyone calls it a footnote.
