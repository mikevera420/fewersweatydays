Most of what people hear about underarm Botox is the appointment itself: the needles, the numbing, and whether it hurts. Less gets said about the months after, when your shirts stay dry for a while and then one regular workday there's a wet patch under your arm again.

This piece follows one round from the day of the shots to the day you book the next one. It covers the first couple of weeks, the stretch when the sweat starts coming back, and the calendar, cost, and insurance side of going again.

## The first couple of weeks after the shots

The appointment is usually short. The clinic may paint your armpit with a test solution first to see where the sweat is coming from. Then you get a grid of small shots, usually about 10 to 20 per armpit, spaced a centimeter or two apart.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> The needle is very fine, and a lot of clinics use numbing cream, a cold spray, or ice to make it easier.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup> It still stings while it's happening, and your armpits can be sore for a bit afterward. Pain from the shots is the most common complaint people have about this treatment.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> Botox is FDA approved for severe underarm sweating, so for a lot of dermatology offices this is a routine visit.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup>

Most people go back to work the same day. The clinic will give you aftercare instructions before you leave, and it makes sense to follow what your own clinic tells you over anything you read online.

Then you wait to see if it worked. People usually notice less sweat within 2 to 4 days.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> That's a typical range, and your own armpits might be a little faster or a little slower.

The first dry shirt might be a light blue button-down you had stopped wearing to work because it showed everything by ten in the morning. You wear it all day. Around lunch you go to the restroom and you're about to lift your arm in front of the mirror to check, and you realize you haven't thought about it all morning. That night you take the shirt off and there's no dark ring under the arms and no white salt line along the seam.

Short-term side effects are usually small. Soreness, a little bruising, and some itching where the shots went in are the common ones. Headache and muscle aches can happen but are rare, and so is sweat showing up in new spots on the body.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> If something seems off after the shots, call the clinic that did them.

## The stretch when the sweat starts coming back

It's a few months after your last round. It's a weekday morning, you're getting dressed for work, and you grab the gray crew-neck out of the drawer without thinking about it. A year ago you kept that shirt at the back of the drawer, because gray shows a sweat patch faster than almost any other color you own. For months now you've been wearing it like any other shirt.

You have a 10:30 meeting. You sit through it, reach across the table for a printout, and don't think about your arms once. Around noon you're standing in line for lunch, you lift your arm to grab a napkin, and you see it: a dark patch under your left arm about the size of your palm. The right side looks fine. You check again in the restroom mirror and it's definitely there, and there's no way it's going to dry before your 2 o'clock call.

The rest of the afternoon goes differently. You keep your elbows close to your sides. You put your jacket on even though the office is warm. You try to remember if there's still a spare shirt in your car, and then you remember you stopped keeping one there back in the spring. On the 2 o'clock call you leave your camera on but you keep your arms down on the desk the whole time.

That evening, for the first time in months, you pick tomorrow's shirt on purpose. The gray one goes back in the drawer. You lay out the black tee instead and put a spare in your work bag. This is what it can look like when the Botox starts wearing off. You go from not thinking about shirts at all to planning around them again, and it can start on one arm and on the busier days before it shows up every day.

How long the dry stretch lasts is different for different people. A review of Botox use in dermatology put underarm relief at about 4 to 9 months on average.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup> Where you land in that range can change from one round to the next.

The shots are meant to wear off. Sweat coming back after a few months is the normal course of this treatment, and you didn't do anything to cause it. Repeat rounds are the standard plan for people who stay with this treatment.

For people who keep going, relief often lasts longer after repeat rounds.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup> One French clinic that looked back through years of its own patient records found that relief tended to last longer after later rounds than after the first.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-3">[3]</a></sup> That won't happen for everyone, and it's still useful to know that your first round can be different from later ones.

## Booking the next round

A lot of people end up going back 1 to 2 times a year.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> So the day after the gray shirt, you open your calendar and look at when your last round was. Then you pull up the clinic's booking page to see how far out the openings are. Dermatology schedules can fill up weeks ahead, and the first open slot might land after the beach trip you were hoping to be dry for.

Some people also time their rounds around the seasons. In Japan, one study of insurance records found that underarm Botox appointments peak in May and June, before the hottest months, which suggests people there book ahead of summer.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-4">[4]</a></sup>

You also need the time away from work. The visit itself doesn't take long, but there's the drive and the waiting room. Some people book the first appointment of the morning so they can be at their desk by ten, and others take a half day when the clinic is across town.

Cost comes up again too. It varies a lot by clinic, by how much medicine your armpits need, and by your insurance, so what a friend paid might have nothing to do with what you pay. Asking the clinic ahead of time what your dose will cost saves you a surprise at the front desk.

In the US, booking can also mean insurance paperwork. Some insurers want proof that you already tried strong prescription antiperspirants before they will cover the shots, and many want prior authorization before the visit. That can mean a letter from the dermatologist's office, some waiting, and sometimes a denial letter in the mail and an appeal. If you've been through it before, keep copies of whatever got it approved last time, since you may need the same paperwork again.

Each round is also a point where you decide whether to keep going. Some people stay on a steady schedule for years. Others take a break because the cost adds up or because they're tired of needles in their armpits. Some want something that lasts longer and start asking their dermatologist about other options. Any of those is a fair thing to talk over at the appointment. This article is general information, and coaching is not medical treatment.

Before your next booking, write down the date you first noticed the sweat coming back. Put it in your phone's notes or right on the calendar, along with which shirts or situations showed it, like the gray shirt at lunch, the left arm before the right, or the meeting where you kept your jacket on. Do that every round. After two or three rounds you'll have your own timeline written down, so you can book the next appointment before the wet patches start instead of after, and the decision about whether to keep going comes from how long your relief actually lasted.
