You open the bathroom drawer, or you scroll your order history on your phone, and there it is: a short stack of things that each helped a little. Clinical-strength antiperspirant, a wipe pack, a powder, a spray you bought at midnight, maybe a box for a device you used for a few weeks. None of that was stupid. Most of it did something. The problem is that "something" kept running out.

## The drawer full of things that each worked a little

Start with the clinical antiperspirant. For a lot of people it cuts underarm wetness on calmer days. Then the skin gets irritated, or you forget a night, or you need another coat before a long meeting and the burn starts. The wipe pack is similar. It can dry a palm or an underarm for a short window. Then you are reapplying in a bathroom stall, or the effect fades before the afternoon is done. Powder can keep fabric from sticking for a while, then leave a chalky feel that needs topping up. The midnight spray felt like a rescue the night you ordered it, and by week two it was one more bottle on the shelf.

Some people add oral pills for a stretch, or try Botox for a site that matters most. Those can help too. They also come with schedules, side effects, cost, and the question of what happens when the window closes. Mike's clients often show up having already tried Drysol, Qbrexza, powders, wipes, orals, and Botox in some mix. That list is ordinary trying: wet hands, wet underarms, or a wet face, and whatever you can buy or get prescribed.

You find something. You use it carefully for a week or two. It works on Tuesday and fails on Thursday. Irritation shows up, or the routine gets hard to keep, or the short window stops matching the day you actually have. So you order the next thing. The drawer fills. Products still have a real place next to medical care. Aluminum chloride antiperspirants are often where clinicians start for a reason.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-1">[1]</a></sup> Initial management can include lifestyle and behavioral recommendations next to that care.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-2">[2]</a></sup> Coaching on this site leaves that drawer in place and looks at a different question: what else is going on on the days the product is not enough.

## What a pattern looks like in one ordinary week

Here is what "pattern" means in plain terms: a normal week written down with the sweaty days marked, tied to sleep, heat, caffeine, stress, meals, and routine changes.

Monday you slept about five hours. You had two coffees before a string of meetings. Hands and underarms were worse by late morning. Tuesday you slept closer to seven, switched the second coffee to tea after noon, and the afternoon was quieter. Wednesday the car was hot at 3 p.m. after you sat in traffic. Face and chest felt worse even though you used the same antiperspirant as Monday. Thursday you had a date or a presentation and the anticipatory stress started mid-afternoon, before you left the house. Friday you ate a heavy, spicy dinner late and noticed overnight and next-morning sweat that felt different from a calm day. Saturday was cooler, fewer plans, one coffee, and the drawer products seemed to "work" again. Sunday you stayed up late on a screen, slept poorly, and Monday looked bad again.

That week cannot prove a single cause, but worse days often line up with sleep, heat, caffeine amount and timing, stress events, meals, and routine changes. Mike has people track a short list on purpose: sugar, stimulants, and stress; how much caffeine and whether it was coffee or tea and when; sleep length; heat; meals; and specific stress events like an interview, public speaking, or a date. Doing that for several weeks is different from testing one product for three days. The short product test tells you whether the bottle helped today. Several weeks of notes tell you which days keep showing up worse, and what else was already happening.

Sweating can also change across a single day. Plenty of people notice a calmer morning, a tougher middle of the day, and a different evening, especially when stress is in the mix.<sup><a class="cite-ref" href="#src-3">[3]</a></sup> That is another reason one afternoon product trial can mislead you. The week has more information than the bottle alone.

## What a coaching conversation looks at that a product can't

A coaching conversation on this site stays out of prescribing and diagnosing. Mike's line for himself is blunt: you're not medical. People are expected to have seen a doctor first to rule out underlying conditions. Coaching sits next to that care rather than replacing it.

Weekly check-ins look at what got written down, which days were worse, whether sleep or caffeine timing moved, and whether a stress event landed before the sweat spike. They also look at what already changed and what is worth trying next for another week. When something looks medical or secondary, the next step is back to a doctor.

Mike often frames the first stretch as a 6-week trial. Weekly check-ins review which variable moved and which one did not. The aim is to see the week clearly enough that the next small change is based on notes instead of another late-night order. There is never an expectation that someone will be cured. The useful frame is fewer sweaty days that feel more manageable, controllable, and predictable through what you can see and adjust, without a promise that the sweating disappears.

A product can dry a site for a window. A coaching week sits with the notes and asks what Monday and Wednesday had in common, or whether the "good" product weeks were also better sleep weeks, or whether a symptom pattern belongs with a clinician. That attention over weeks, with scope kept honest, is what the conversation is for.

For the next seven days, mark which days felt worse and write one line about what else happened: sleep, heat, meetings, caffeine, meals, routine change. That is the same kind of noticing a coaching week starts with.
